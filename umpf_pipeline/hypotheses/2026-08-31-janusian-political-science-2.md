## 1. The Domain

In political science, coalition government formation occurs when multiple political parties come together to create a government, often due to no single party winning an outright majority in elections. This process involves negotiation, compromise, and strategic alignment of diverse political agendas.

## 2. The Proposition

Coalition governments are formed through consensus-building among parties, requiring compromise on key policy issues.

## 3. The Inversion

The exact opposite is true: Coalition governments are formed through conflict and competition among parties, where each party seeks to impose its agenda without compromise.

## 4. The Simultaneous Hold

> "Coalition governments are formed through consensus-building among parties, requiring compromise on key policy issues."
> "Coalition governments are formed through conflict and competition among parties, where each party seeks to impose its agenda without compromise."
> "Both are true simultaneously."

- **(A) Compromise**: Coalition governments are formed through varying degrees of consensus and conflict, depending on the specific political context.
- **(B) Synthesis**: Coalition governments can be understood as a blend of consensus and conflict, where parties negotiate but also maintain their distinct agendas.
- **(C) Paradox**: Coalition governments are formed through both consensus and conflict simultaneously; parties negotiate to reach agreements while simultaneously competing to assert their individual agendas.

(A) fails because it suggests a sliding scale of consensus and conflict rather than holding both simultaneously; (B) fails because it implies a resolution that dilutes the inherent tension between cooperation and competition. (C) holds because both consensus and conflict can coexist in the formation of a coalition, affecting the same negotiation process at the same time.

## 5. The Hypothesis (The Third Thing)

1. **Simultaneous-hold sentence (required):** Both consensus-building and conflict-driven competition are true simultaneously for the same coalition government formation; the theory must contain both.
2. **Falsifiable prediction:** If both consensus-building and conflict-driven competition hold simultaneously, then we would expect to see instances where coalition agreements reflect both collaborative policy-making and significant dissent among parties — which would not be predicted by viewing coalition formation as solely consensus-driven or conflict-driven alone.
