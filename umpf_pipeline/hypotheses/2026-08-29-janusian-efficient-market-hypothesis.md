## 1. The Domain

The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH) posits that financial markets are "informationally efficient," meaning that asset prices reflect all available information at any given time. Consequently, it is impossible to consistently achieve higher returns than the overall market through expert stock selection or market timing.

## 2. The Proposition

The load-bearing assumption is: "Financial markets are efficient, and prices reflect all available information."

## 3. The Inversion

The exact opposite is true: "Financial markets are inefficient, and prices do not reflect all available information."

## 4. The Simultaneous Hold

> "Financial markets are efficient, and prices reflect all available information."  
> "Financial markets are inefficient, and prices do not reflect all available information."  
> "Both are true simultaneously."

- **(A) Compromise**: "Markets are sometimes efficient and sometimes inefficient depending on the context or time period."
- **(B) Synthesis**: "Markets can be efficient in the long term but inefficient in the short term due to behavioral biases."
- **(C) Paradox**: "Markets can be both efficient and inefficient at the same time, depending on the type of information and the market participants involved."

(C) is the genuine paradox because it holds that both efficiency and inefficiency can coexist in the same market environment simultaneously, while (A) and (B) fail to be Janusian as they resolve the contradiction by suggesting different contexts rather than holding both states at once.

## 5. The Hypothesis (The Third Thing)

**"If both financial markets are efficient and inefficient simultaneously, then certain market anomalies will persist while others will disappear — which would not be predicted by either truth held alone."**
