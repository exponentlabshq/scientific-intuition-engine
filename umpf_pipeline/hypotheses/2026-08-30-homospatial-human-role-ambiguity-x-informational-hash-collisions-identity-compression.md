## 1. The Two Source Entities

**Entity A — Human Role Ambiguity**: Human role ambiguity occurs when an individual's responsibilities, authority, or organizational position are insufficiently differentiated. A single person may simultaneously occupy multiple functional positions, or multiple actors may be treated as interchangeable with respect to a responsibility that actually requires differentiation. The underlying problem is not simply "confusion" — it is that distinct organizational states can collapse into an insufficiently differentiated representation of who does what.

**Entity B — Informational Hash Collisions**: A hash function maps an input space into a finite output space, H: X → Y. Because multiple inputs can map to the same output (x₁ ≠ x₂ while H(x₁) = H(x₂)), distinct underlying information can become indistinguishable when represented solely by the hash value. The collision is a consequence of representational compression: distinctions existing in the underlying state are lost at the representation layer.

## 2. The Superimposition

Force both domains into the same conceptual slot: **distinct underlying states → shared representation**. In organizational systems, multiple distinct responsibilities, authorities, or role configurations can be compressed into a single poorly differentiated role description, title, workflow state, or accountability channel. In information systems, multiple distinct inputs can be compressed into the same finite identifier. The common structure is not that both systems are "ambiguous" — it is that a representational system can collapse distinctions that exist in the underlying state, making otherwise different states observationally indistinguishable at the level of the representation. This produces a general phenomenon of **identity compression**: the representation remains valid as a representation, but becomes insufficient to uniquely recover the state that generated it.

## 3. The Emergent Third Thing

The emergent construct is **Identity Compression**: S₁ ≠ S₂ ∧ R(S₁) = R(S₂), where S is an underlying state and R is the representational system. Identity compression occurs when the representational layer preserves insufficient information to distinguish states that are materially different at the underlying layer — underlying distinction ≠ representational distinction. The construct applies independently of whether the underlying states are people, responsibilities, records, objects, or information.

**Organizational instantiation**: two materially different accountability states — S₁ = "manager has decision authority," S₂ = "manager provides advice but has no decision authority" — both represented operationally as R(S₁) = R(S₂) = "manager responsible." The organization has compressed a meaningful distinction into a common identifier; the resulting ambiguity is a property of the representation, not merely psychological.

**Information-theoretic instantiation**: x₁ ≠ x₂ while H(x₁) = H(x₂) means the representation alone cannot distinguish the underlying inputs. Both systems instantiate the same abstract relation: many possible states → one observable representation.

## 4. The Hypothesis

**Fusion sentence**: I force human role ambiguity and informational hash collisions into the same conceptual slot until Identity Compression emerges: distinct underlying states become observationally indistinguishable because the representation preserves insufficient information to distinguish them.

**Falsifiable prediction**: If Identity Compression is a genuine cross-domain mechanism, then organizational environments with greater role-state compression — measured by the number of materially distinct responsibilities, authorities, or accountability states represented by the same formal role, workflow label, or ownership identifier — will exhibit higher rates of downstream identification errors than environments in which those states are explicitly differentiated. Formally: IC↑ ⇒ E_identification↑, where IC = degree of identity compression and E_identification = rate of downstream identification/accountability errors. The hypothesis is rejected if increased representational compression does NOT predict increased identification errors after controlling for organizational complexity and other relevant variables.

## 5. Operationalization

**A. State multiplicity**: M = (distinct underlying states) / (representational identifiers). Higher M indicates greater compression.
**B. Distinguishability loss**: D = 1 − (preserved distinctions / material distinctions).
**C. Downstream error**: the frequency with which actors or systems incorrectly infer the underlying state from the compressed representation — incorrect assignment of responsibility, duplicated or omitted work, escalation to the wrong authority, incorrect data attribution, mistaken ownership, failed handoffs, incorrect access/permission decisions. Central empirical relationship: D↑ ⇒ E_downstream↑.

## 6. Why This Is Different From a Simple Analogy

The original construction treated the two domains as sharing the property of "ambiguity" — too weak. Identity Compression instead identifies a structural transformation common to both: high-dimensional underlying state → lower-dimensional representation. The hypothesis does not require human roles to literally behave like hash functions. It predicts that the same representational failure principle can appear in both systems: distinctions that exist in the underlying state disappear at the representation layer. This is the proposed homospatial invariant.

## 7. Boundary Conditions

The hypothesis does not claim that every case of role ambiguity is equivalent to a hash collision, nor that human organizations literally implement cryptographic hashing. A genuine hash collision has precise mathematical properties; organizational role compression need not. The claim is narrower: both systems can instantiate the same abstract representational failure — multiple materially distinct states mapping to an insufficiently differentiated representation. The analogy fails if organizational ambiguity cannot be characterized as a loss of state distinguishability, or if increased compression produces no measurable increase in downstream identification error.
